01 Where this came from

I kept typing the same five things.

Not domain knowledge — just method, re-supplied by hand every session:

  • Investigate before you change anything.
  • Reproduce it first — don’t guess at the cause.
  • Write the failing test before the fix.
  • You said it works. Show me the output.
  • That was one step, not the whole task. Keep going.

So I wrote it down: named blocks of work, each with its steps and a definition of done. Then, for my own amusement, I named them in Latin.

That turned out to be the part that mattered.

02 The thing I didn’t expect

“Fix” is the most crowded word in your repository.

Here is how I think about it. An agent works entirely in text, and nearly all of that text is programming vocabulary — the files it reads, the stack trace, the diff and the commit messages it writes, the test output it reads back, and the request I typed. Every one of those words is a triangle: fix, check, test, build, commit, refactor, done. It reads and writes thousands of them in a session. My request is one more triangle, and should be: it is a programming task, and programming tasks are written in programming words — fix the null deref in the session handler. What must not be a triangle is the framework that governs that work — the names of its blocks and its boundaries. Name those in programming words too and they dissolve into the pile.

So make the framework names squares. The words of the work stay triangles, my request stays a triangle, and only the layer that governs them changes shape. That is the entire trick, and it costs nothing.

Concretely, this is how crowded a triangle gets. Picture a routine session: I ask for that null deref, and before my framework says a word the agent has already met fix five times — the branch it checked out, a run of commits, a stale FIXME, a couple of test names, and my own sentence. Then my framework arrives and says it a sixth time:

That is the whole observation. Everything below is what follows from it — and, at the end, the file where it lives.

These are boundary words — words whose only job is to mark where the framework ends and the work begins. Mine happen to be Latin. Emendatio for a repair, Restitutio for a broken build, Purgatio for an analyser alert. Half a joke that stuck — those words appear in no repository I have touched, and the model still knows what they mean. The Latin is incidental. The boundary is the point. Three is a sample: my own set runs to eight kinds of work, each with its own words for the phases, the proofs and the ending.

03 How it sits around the work

A layer of words your code never says.

The framework doesn’t replace anything or sit inside your language. It wraps it. Inside the blocks, everything is ordinary — your files, your commands, your commit messages. The edges are made of vocabulary that can’t be confused with the contents.

I never type a boundary word at the agent. I say “the login endpoint 500s on an empty body.” They live inside the skill file, where only the agent reads them. What I get back is a transcript with visible seams.

Why the seams matter

One more thing I did not expect. I assumed the vocabulary would be a substitute for writing the plan out. It behaved more like a stronger version of it — and when I wrote the plan out as well, the two did not cancel or repeat. They compounded. The names hold the shape; the plan fills it in.

04 Picking the words

A boundary word doesn’t have to be Latin. It does have to be disjoint.

The requirement is only that the name not collide with the material it governs. But you can miss in two directions, and the shapes make it obvious which is which.

Why Latin lands in the middle band is a guess, not a finding: rare in source code, abundant in training data, still transparent — emendatio already means correction, so the name carries weight before I define anything. But that is me explaining my own result after the fact. Old Norse, liturgical terms, climbing vocabulary, chess notation — anything with real meaning your repository has never had reason to write down should do the same job. If you try one, tell me what happened.

06 Two layers

The strange words only go on the rules.

Lex Termini is the rule that decides when a block of work is allowed to end. Mend is what you do to the bug. One of those is Latin. The other is the most ordinary word I could have picked.

That holds all the way through. Every rule in my framework has a strange name. Almost every action has a plain English one. I wrote eight of these files and never once noticed I was doing it.

Once I saw it, the reason was obvious: the two names are for opposite things.

A rule name gets looked up. Lex Recordi appears fifteen times, and every one of them has to point at the same rule. A word the model already holds opinions about will drift toward them. So I want one it holds no opinions about at all.

An action name gets carried out. Reproduce. Pin. Cleanse. Here I want every opinion the model has — those opinions are what tell it what to do. A strange word would only get in the way.

Systems people have a name for this shape: the control plane and the data plane. You keep the channel that carries instructions separate from the channel that carries payload, so payload can never be read as instruction. Prompt injection is precisely that separation failing. I am borrowing the picture, not claiming the research — but it describes the file better than “Latin is better” ever did.

Which gives me a prediction I did not want: more boundary words would make this worse, not better. Put them on the actions and I am teaching the model a private dialect for nothing. They pay their way on the names that get looked up, and nowhere else.

Where it doesn’t hold

Praeco breaks it outright — a boundary word sitting on an action (announce the step before you take it), twenty times over. Witness / Testes gives a single step both an English name for the doing and a strange one for the standard it must meet; I think that is the pattern at its sharpest rather than a hole in it, but that is me arguing my own case. And the rite names — Emendatio is simply Latin for repair — sit squarely on the line.

There is a duller explanation I cannot rule out, either. Names you look up should be unambiguous is just good naming, and it is as true of variables as of this. Perhaps I have rediscovered that and put a toga on it.

But the test is cheap, and far narrower than anything else on this page: rename the seventeen rules into English — Lex Recordi becomes Memory Rule — and change nothing else at all. One thing varies. If the gates hold anyway, this section is wrong.

07 Evidence

What is evidence, and what is still my hunch.

Two different claims live on this page, and they are not equally supported. One has published work behind it. The other is mine, and untested. Mixing them would be the fastest way to lose an argument I think is worth having, so here they are, separated.

Supported: a gate the model cannot talk its way through

The strongest part of the framework is not a word. It is the rule that a repair needs a test you watched fail before it may be called mended — and that Testes means the suite’s output, not the model’s opinion of the suite.

That rule is doing real work, and the reason is documented. Asked to check its own reasoning without an external signal, a model does not get better — it gets worse, and keeps getting worse. GPT‑4 on GSM8K fell from 95.5% to 91.5% after one round of self‑correction, and to 89.0% after two. Earlier results suggesting otherwise had quietly used the right answer to decide when to stop correcting.1

So “verify your work” asks for the one thing the literature says is unreliable. sara verify asks for an exit code. That distinction is defensible today, in any vocabulary, in any language.

Adjacent, but weaker than it looks

Models genuinely are trained to rank instructions by privilege — system above developer, developer above user, user above tool output.2 It is tempting to reach for that as proof of a vocabulary of authority. It is not. That hierarchy keys on where a message came from, never on which words it used. It shows layered authority is real. It says nothing about whether rare words create one.

Evidence pointing the other way

Rarity has a cost. Tokens a model saw too little of during training carry poorly‑formed representations and behave erratically.3 If isolation were free, the ideal boundary word would be a random string — and it clearly is not. That bounds the claim: a boundary word must be rare in your repository while remaining common in the training data. Latin qualifies because of law, medicine and science, not because it is old — and plenty of non‑Latin words qualify too.

The honest summary cuts against this page’s emphasis. The published work supports the structure — external gates, proof over assertion, state kept outside the model. On boundary words it is silent at best and cautionary at worst. My hunch is that they add something on top of the structure. That part is a hypothesis wearing a website.

What would change my mind

Replace every boundary word with an ordinary one — Repair for Emendatio, Verify for Testes — keep every gate exactly as it is, and run the same work. If the results hold, the structure was the whole finding and the boundary was decoration — and I would rather publish that than defend the nicer story. I have not run it yet, so nothing on this page should be read as though I had.
